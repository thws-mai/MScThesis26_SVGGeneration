\chapter{Introduction}\label{ch:Introduction}


Vector graphics differ fundamentally from raster images. Raster images represent visual content as grids of pixels, where each pixel stores color information without explicit semantic meaning. In contrast, vector graphics describe images using mathematical primitives such as lines, curves, shapes, and groups. Because these primitives are defined parametrically, vector graphics can be scaled, transformed, and edited without loss of quality.

Among vector formats, Scalable Vector Graphics (SVG) has become a widely adopted standard. SVG is a text-based representation that encodes geometry, styling, and hierarchical relationships between graphic elements. As a structured format, SVG allows graphics to be programmatically generated and modified, which makes it particularly suitable for computational design workflows.

From the perspective of generative modeling, these structural properties introduce additional challenges. Unlike raster images, SVG graphics explicitly encode geometry and topology. As a result, generating SVG graphics requires more than visual similarity. Generated outputs must also maintain structural correctness so that they remain interpretable. Graphics that contain redundant paths, broken hierarchy, or unstable geometry may appear visually plausible while being difficult to reuse in practical settings.

!?Before learning-based approaches, vector graphics were primarily produced through algorithmic vectorization techniques that approximate raster images using geometric primitives. Although such methods remain widely used, they rely on heuristic rules and often struggle with complex scenes, stylized content, or semantic guidance from text. The emergence of learning-based approaches introduces new possibilities but also raises questions about how models construct vector structure internally.


\section{Motivation}

In recent years, generative models have significantly transformed the landscape of visual design and digital content creation. Diffusion models, large-scale multimodal systems such as CLIP, and transformer-based architectures have demonstrated that high-quality image synthesis from text is no longer a research prototype but a practical reality. Systems such as Stable Diffusion, DALL-E, and Midjourney are capable of producing visually convincing raster images that are sometimes difficult to distinguish from human-created content. Continuous improvements in realism, controllability, and semantic alignment have accelerated the adoption of generative models in creative workflows.

However, this progress has been much more pronounced in the raster image domain than in vector graphics. While research on vector graphics generation has grown rapidly, the reliability, controllability, and structural quality of generated vector graphics remain open challenges. At the same time, vector graphics continue to play a central role in modern digital design. Icons, logos, illustrations, user interface elements, and data visualizations are typically created and maintained in vector formats because they must remain editable and resolution independent.

This importance is reflected not only in research but also in industry. Design tools such as Adobe Illustrator and Figma rely heavily on vector representations as the core format for interactive editing. Recent developments in design copilots and AI-assisted creative tools further highlight a shift toward generation systems that must produce editable graphics rather than static images. Emerging integrations between generative models and design platforms suggest that future workflows will require generation methods capable of producing structurally meaningful vector graphics that can be directly reused and modified by designers.

These trends motivate the study of SVG generation not only as a generative modeling task but also as a representation problem, where the structure of the output is as important as its visual appearance.


\section{Research Problem}

Research on SVG generation has expanded across multiple directions, including sequence-based models, optimization-driven approaches, neural representations, and multimodal generation pipelines. These methods differ significantly in their internal representations, the supervision signals used during training or optimization, and the mechanisms through which these signals are translated into final SVG outputs. Representation choices influence optimization stability, geometric consistency, and editability, yet these relationships remain insufficiently understood.

A central difficulty is that many works emphasize visual similarity between generated outputs and reference images while structural properties of SVG graphics receive less systematic analysis. Generated graphics may achieve strong perceptual quality but still exhibit issues such as fragmented geometry, redundant paths, missing hierarchy, or limited editability. This creates a gap between visually convincing generation and practically usable vector graphics.

In addition, evaluation practices for SVG generation remain inconsistent. Quantitative metrics commonly adapted from raster image synthesis, such as FID or reconstruction-based losses, primarily measure visual similarity rather than structural quality. While these metrics provide useful signals, they do not capture properties such as geometric consistency, topology, hierarchy, or editability. Consequently, quantitative results reported across different models may not fully reflect practical SVG quality, making comparison between approaches difficult.

Furthermore, the diversity of internal representations — including parametric primitives, command sequences, latent embeddings, and neural implicit functions — complicates understanding how architectural design influences structural outcomes. There is currently no unified perspective that explains how representation and optimization strategies relate to the resulting vector structure.

This lack of systematic analysis of representation, structure, and evaluation constitutes the central research gap addressed in this thesis.

\section{Thesis Objective}

This thesis investigates SVG generation and vectorization methods from the perspective of representation, optimization, and structural quality. Rather than proposing a new generation model, the goal is to develop a structured understanding of how existing architectures construct vector graphics and how these design choices influence the resulting SVG structure.

Particular emphasis is placed on models whose generation process remains interpretable in terms of vector operations or clearly defined architectural components. While recent large language model–based approaches treat SVG generation as structured code synthesis, their internal generation process is less explicit with respect to geometric construction. For this reason, language-model-based SVG generation is considered primarily as an emerging direction, while the main analysis focuses on optimization-based, neural, and hybrid approaches where representation choices can be directly examined.

To support systematic analysis, the thesis focuses on a selected set of open-source SVG generation models with available implementations and published descriptions. This enables comparative study across methods that differ in representation strategy, supervision signals, and optimization pipelines. The analysis aims to clarify how different approaches balance visual fidelity with structural correctness and to identify evaluation criteria relevant to real-world design workflows.

These methods formulate SVG generation as code synthesis, leveraging transformer architectures pretrained on large-scale text and multimodal data. The works such as Starvector, OmniSVG, LLM4SVG, LLM4SVG and InternSVG adopt MLLM designs that integrate visual encoders, based on Vision Transformers or CLIP-style approaches. Building on this idea, Language model-based pipelines work on SVG as a multimodal sequence that encodes both semantic and structural information. In this paradigm, SVGs are treated similarly to natural language, where models generate XML commands while conditioning on textual or visual inputs. Compared to earlier sequence-based models, these models focus on multimodal alignment and ability to handle multiple task, such as understanding, generation and editing. Despite their high-level results, length nature of SVG code makes it difficult for transformer models to maintain structural consistency over long sequences. In addition, these models are also less interpretable, in terms of generation process, which is distributed across large neural architectures rather than explicitly defined vector operations. For this reason, in this thesis, we did not focus on language-based model SVG generation. Instead, we focus on approaches where architectures provide clearer insight into how vector graphics are constructed and optimized.

\section{Research Goals}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item To explain and analyze the core properties, structures, and components of SVG graphics as a foundation for understanding their representation and generation.
    \item To collect, review, and categorize existing open-source methods for SVG generation across various input modalities.
    \item To investigate the types of deep learning and machine learning architectures employed in SVG generation models and analyze their design principles.
    \item To examine how these architectures represent SVG data internally and how their outputs are transformed into standard SVG files.
    \item To explore and evaluate existing approaches, metrics, and benchmarks for assessing the quality of generated SVGs.
    \item To assess and compare the output quality of current SVG generation models based on the identified evaluation criteria.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Research Questions}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the main properties and structures that define an editable and high-quality SVG?        
    \item What open-source methods currently exist for generating SVGs from different input modalities?
    \item What types of deep learning and machine learning architectures are models used for SVG generation?
    \item What kinds of structure do these models produce?
    \item How can the quality and editability of generated SVG be evaluated?
    \item What is the observed quality of SVG outputs across different models?
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this thesis builds on the conceptual progression introduced above. The background chapter introduces the fundamental concepts required to understand vector graphics generation, including SVG structure, internal representation types, differentiable rendering, and supervision strategies used in learning-based methods.

Building on this foundation, subsequent chapters review existing SVG generation approaches and analyze how architectural design choices influence vector structure. Particular attention is given to the relationship between representation strategy and the properties of generated graphics.

Following the architectural analysis, the thesis introduces evaluation perspectives for SVG generation that extend beyond visual similarity. Both quantitative and qualitative criteria are considered, including measures related to editability, geometric consistency, and practical usability.

These evaluation perspectives are then applied in a comparative analysis of selected open-source models, where strengths, limitations, and trade-offs between approaches are examined. The thesis concludes by summarizing key insights, discussing limitations, and outlining directions for future research in structured vector graphics generation.
